How to actually study Science

The gap between AL3 and AL1 is not knowing more. It is knowing more precisely, more causally, and more structurally. These strategies are evidence-backed (Bjork, Clear, Dweck, Gollwitzer) and aimed at exactly that gap.

Memory and recall

1. Retrieve, don't reread

Re-reading the Learn tab feels productive and is one of the weakest things you can do. Retrieval — trying to remember with the page closed — is the strongest. Read a topic once, close it, write down the key ideas from memory, then check what you missed. The gaps you find are the whole point.

Try this now → Vocab Drill → Flashcards

2. Space it out

A definition drilled five times today is gone by next week. The same definition met today, tomorrow, in two days, in four, in a week, will stay. The app schedules this for you: items you find easy come back rarely, items you miss come back tomorrow.

Try this now → Review queue → Flashcards

3. Interleave topics

A whole session on Circuits feels smooth, and that smoothness is misleading. Mix two or three topics per session. It feels harder, which is exactly why it works: your brain has to choose which principle applies, which is what the exam actually asks.

Writing answers that score

4. Explain the middle, not just the ends

This is the single biggest difference between AL3 and AL1. A weak answer says A happens, so C happens. A strong answer explains B — the mechanism connecting them. "The water evaporates, so he feels cold" is AL3. "The water evaporates, and evaporation takes heat from his skin, so he feels cold" is AL1.

Try this now → Structured Answers

5. Use C-E-R every single time

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning. State what is happening, quote what in the question tells you so, then explain the science that links them. Not because it is a nice format, but because examiners award marks for exactly those three things, and a scaffold stops you dropping one under time pressure.

Try this now → Structured Answers → PSLE Tips

6. Decode the command word before you write a word

Describe, explain, infer, predict, suggest and compare want different shapes of answer. Writing a description when the stem said "explain" loses marks even when every fact is right. Read the command word, then decide the shape, then write.

Try this now → Command Words

7. Swap fuzzy verbs for scientific ones

"Makes", "gets", "changes" and "goes" are where marks quietly disappear. The precise word is almost always available: absorbs, transfers, condenses, evaporates, photosynthesises, dissolves. When you catch yourself writing a fuzzy verb, stop and name the process.

8. The Feynman test

If you cannot explain it in plain words, you do not understand it yet. Explain a topic as if to a younger child. The exact sentence where you stumble is the gap. Go back and fill that one thing rather than rereading the whole topic.

Seeing the whole subject

9. Learn the network, not the list

Topics are not sixteen separate boxes. Evaporation belongs to Cycles and to Energy. Adaptations belong to Diversity and to Interactions. Novel exam scenarios stop being frightening once you have seen how the pieces connect, because you recognise the principle instead of hunting for the example.

Try this now → Mega Map

10. Build the map yourself, then check it

Reading someone else's concept map teaches very little. Drawing your own forces you to decide what causes what, and the places you hesitate are the places you do not really know. Build it, then let the Coach tell you what is missing.

Time and energy

11. Show up, even when you don't want to

Missing one day is noise. Missing two in a row is how stopping begins. On a bad day, do ten minutes of something you have already mastered. Showing up is the win; the content is a bonus.

Try this now → Compass

12. Set one intention before you start

"In the next twenty minutes I will do two open-ended questions on Energy" roughly doubles the chance you actually do it, compared with "I'll do some science". Name the time, the task and the topic before you begin.

Try this now → Compass

13. Sleep on it

Memory is consolidated during sleep, not during revision. Eight hours tonight does more for tomorrow's recall than one more hour now. A tired brain learns poorly and remembers worse, so the extra hour often costs more than it adds.

Mindset

14. Praise the strategy, not the ability

"I used C-E-R and checked my command word" is more useful than "I'm good at science". The first can be repeated tomorrow. The second collapses the first time something is hard.

15. Nerves are fuel, not a warning

A fast heartbeat before a paper is your body moving blood and oxygen to your brain. Naming it as preparation rather than fear measurably improves how people perform. The feeling is not the problem; the story you tell about it is.

16. The Mirror tells the truth

A single mastery percentage hides more than it shows. The Mirror separates what is consolidated, what is about to be forgotten, and where your time actually went. If your hours do not match your weakest theme, it will say so plainly.

Paper tactics

17. Paper 1 — answer every MCQ, never leave a blank

There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so a blank is a guaranteed zero where a considered guess is not. Eliminate the two options you can rule out on principle, then choose between what remains.

Try this now → PSLE Practice mode

18. Read the number of marks before you write

Two marks means two distinct scoring points, not one point said twice at length. Count your points against the marks before moving on. Most lost marks in open-ended questions are missing points, not wrong ones.

Try this now → Structured Answers

19. In experiment questions, name the variables out loud

What was changed, what was measured, what was kept the same. Saying all three before answering catches the classic trap of describing a result without identifying what actually caused it.

Try this now → a Simulate tab

20. Quote the question back in your answer

If the stem is about a metal spoon in hot soup, your answer says "the metal spoon", not "the object". Using the question's own nouns proves you are answering this scenario rather than reciting a memorised sentence, and it is the cheapest mark in the paper.
